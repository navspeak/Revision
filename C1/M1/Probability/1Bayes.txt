BAYES' THEOREM
==============

Where it comes from
-------------------
You already know:

  P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B|A)      ... (1)
  P(A and B) = P(B) × P(A|B)      ... (2)

Since both equal P(A and B), set them equal:

  P(B) × P(A|B) = P(A) × P(B|A)

Rearrange to solve for P(A|B):

  P(A|B) = P(A) × P(B|A)
            ──────────────
                P(B)

That is Bayes' Theorem. Nothing new — just conditional probability rearranged.


The Formula
-----------

  P(A|B) = P(A) × P(B|A)
            ──────────────
                P(B)


The Four Components (by name)
------------------------------

  ┌─────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Term            │ Meaning                                                      │
  ├─────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ P(A|B)          │ Posterior — what you want to find. Probability of the cause  │
  │                 │ AFTER you've seen the evidence.                              │
  ├─────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ P(A)            │ Prior — your belief about the cause BEFORE seeing evidence.  │
  ├─────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ P(B|A)          │ Likelihood — how probable is the evidence IF the cause is    │
  │                 │ true?                                                        │
  ├─────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ P(B)            │ Evidence (Marginal) — total probability of seeing this       │
  │                 │ evidence regardless of cause. Computed via Total Probability │
  └─────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  In words:

  Posterior = (Prior × Likelihood) / Evidence


The Law of Total Probability (how to compute P(B))
----------------------------------------------------
When there are multiple mutually exclusive causes (X, Y, Z...) that cover all possibilities:

  P(B) = P(B|X)×P(X) + P(B|Y)×P(Y) + P(B|Z)×P(Z) + ...

  For just two causes:
  P(B) = P(B|A)×P(A) + P(B|not A)×P(not A)

This is always the denominator in Bayes. It is the weighted average of the
likelihood across all possible causes.


Full Bayes Template (two causes)
----------------------------------

  P(A|B) =           P(A) × P(B|A)
            ─────────────────────────────────────────────
            P(A) × P(B|A)  +  P(not A) × P(B|not A)


When to use Bayes
------------------
Use Bayes when:
- You observe an EFFECT and want to find the probability of the CAUSE.
- The question says "given it is defective / positive / true ... what is the
  probability it came from X / has disease / is spam?"

Signal words: "given that it is ..., what is the probability it came from ..."


Reading the problem — what maps to what
-----------------------------------------

  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Problem gives you                         │ Maps to                        │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┤
  │ "Machine X makes 60% of items"            │ P(X) = 0.6   ← Prior          │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┤
  │ "Machine X has a 2% defect rate"          │ P(D|X) = 0.02 ← Likelihood    │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┤
  │ "Item is defective. Where did it come     │ P(X|D) = ?  ← Posterior       │
  │  from?"                                   │                (what you find) │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Total probability of any defective item   │ P(D) = ?   ← Evidence         │
  │ (you compute this)                        │                (denominator)   │
  └───────────────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┘


Step-by-step approach for any Bayes problem
--------------------------------------------
1. Identify all causes and their priors:      P(X), P(Y), ...
2. Identify the likelihoods:                  P(evidence | each cause)
3. Compute joint probabilities (numerators):  P(X) × P(D|X), P(Y) × P(D|Y), ...
4. Sum all joints → that is P(D):             denominator
5. Divide the cause's joint by P(D):          P(X|D) = joint_X / P(D)

Note: all numerators must sum to 1 when divided by P(D). Use this as a sanity check.


Worked Example (factory problem)
----------------------------------
  Machine X: P(X) = 0.6, P(D|X) = 0.02
  Machine Y: P(Y) = 0.4, P(D|Y) = 0.05
  Find: P(Y|D)

  Step 1 — joints (numerators):
    P(X and D) = 0.6 × 0.02 = 0.012
    P(Y and D) = 0.4 × 0.05 = 0.020

  Step 2 — total P(D):
    P(D) = 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032

  Step 3 — posteriors:
    P(X|D) = 0.012 / 0.032 = 37.5%
    P(Y|D) = 0.020 / 0.032 = 62.5%

  Sanity check: 37.5% + 62.5% = 100% ✓

  Interpretation: Machine Y only produces 40% of items, but because its defect
  rate is much higher, a defective item is more likely (62.5%) to have come
  from Y than X.


Key Intuition
--------------
Bayes updates your belief. You start with a prior (what you thought before).
Evidence arrives. Bayes tells you how much to shift your belief given that evidence.

  Prior belief  →  see evidence  →  Posterior belief

High likelihood means the evidence strongly "votes" for that cause. The prior
acts as a weight — a rare cause needs very strong evidence to become probable.


The parallel is:                                                                                                                                                                                                       
   
  Bayes                          Hypothesis Testing                                                                                                                                                                      
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────                                                                                                                                                                      
  Prior P(Disease)           →   Base rate / null hypothesis
  False positive rate 5%     →   Significance level α (p < 0.05)                                                                                                                                                         
  P(Disease | Test Positive) →   What you actually want to know                                                                                                                                                          
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  The exact same problem shows up in hypothesis testing. 
  When you get p < 0.05, most people think "95% chance my result is real." 
  But that's wrong — just like thinking "95% accurate test = 95% chance you're sick."    
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  The true probability your result is real depends on:                                                                                                                                                                   
  - How likely the effect was to exist before you ran the experiment (the prior)
  - Your false positive rate (α)                                                                                                                                                                                         
  - Your test's power (true positive rate, like the 95% in Q1)
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  This is called the False Discovery Rate problem — if you test 100 unlikely hypotheses and use p < 0.05, you'd expect 5 false positives just by chance. 
  If only a few hypotheses were actually true, most of your       
  "significant" results are noise.                                                                                                                                                                                       
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  It's why the replication crisis in science happened — researchers ran tests without accounting for the base rate of true effects in their field, same error as ignoring the 1% disease prevalence.                     
                                                            
  So yes — Bayes and hypothesis testing are the same underlying idea, just framed differently.
