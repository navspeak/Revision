DISCRETE PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTIONS
====================================

Topics Covered
---------------
1. Uniform Distribution      — all outcomes equally likely
2. Binomial Distribution     — n independent trials, probability p of success each
3. Cumulative Probability    — P(X ≤ k), foundation for continuous distributions

Prerequisites
--------------
- Multiplication rule and Addition rule (covered in C1 basics)
- Combinatorics — nCr (n choose r):

  nCr = n! / (r! × (n-r)!)

  Counts the number of ways to get exactly r successes in n trials.
  This is the core of the Binomial formula.


KEY CONCEPTS
=============

1. Addition Rule — Mutually Exclusive vs Independent
------------------------------------------------------
For ANY two events:
  P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)

For MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE events only (P(A and B) = 0):
  P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)

Common mistake: applying the simplified version to independent events.
Independent ≠ Mutually Exclusive.

  Independent events — both CAN happen simultaneously, one doesn't affect the other.
  Example: Tossing a coin AND rolling a die.
    P(Heads) = 1/2,  P(rolling 6) = 1/6
    P(Heads AND 6) = 1/12  ← not zero, so NOT mutually exclusive
    P(Heads or 6) = 1/2 + 1/6 - 1/12 = 7/12   ← must subtract overlap

  Mutually exclusive — cannot happen at the same time, P(A and B) = 0.
  Example: Rolling a 3 or a 6 on a single die.
    Can't get both on one roll → P(3 AND 6) = 0
    P(3 or 6) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3   ← simplified rule valid here

  The trap: "independent" feels like the events are unrelated, so people drop
  the overlap term. But independent only means one doesn't influence the other
  — they can still co-occur.


2. Observed vs Theoretical Probability Distribution
----------------------------------------------------
  Theoretical  — calculated from math/logic, no experiment needed, always exact
  Observed     — counted from actual experiments, varies with sample size

  Example: P(Heads) = 0.5 theoretically.
           Flip 10 times → could get 6 Heads → observed = 0.6
           Flip 10,000 times → observed converges toward 0.5

  The gap exists because of randomness in small samples.


3. Law of Large Numbers
------------------------
As the number of experiments increases, the observed (experimental)
probability distribution converges to the theoretical distribution.

  n → ∞  :  observed probability → theoretical probability

Roulette example: one spin could win, but over 10,000 spins the average
converges to -₹2.70 per spin. Same principle.
