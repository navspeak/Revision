  ========================

  For independent events:                                                                                                                                                                 
  P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
                                                                                                                                                                                          
  For dependent events:
  P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B|A)                                                                                                                                                              
  "Probability of A happening, then B happening given A already happened."
                                                                                                                                                                                          
  ---                                                                                                                                                                                     
  Example — Q2 from above (without replacement):
                                                                                                                                                                                          
  - P(1st red) = 4/10
  - P(2nd red | 1st was red) = 3/9 ← only 3 red left out of 9 remaining                                                                                                                   
                                                                                                                                                                                          
  P(both red) = 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15 ≈ 13.3%                                                                                                                                         
                                                                                                                                                                                          
  Compare with with replacement (independent):                                                                                                                                            
  P(both red) = 4/10 × 4/10 = 16/100 = 4/25 = 16%

  Q2. A bag has 4 red and 6 blue balls. You draw one ball without replacement, then draw another.                                                                                         
  What is the probability the second ball is red, given the first was red?  
  ===========================================================================================    
 "the second ball is red, given the first was red"  => P(2nd Red | 1st Red) = 3/9    
                                                                                    
    Key distinction:                                                                                                                                                                        
                  
  ┌──────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────┐
  │       Question       │              Formula              │       Answer       │                                                                                                       
  ├──────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────┤
  │ P(both red)          │ P(1st red) × P(2nd red | 1st red) │ 4/10 × 3/9 = 13.3% │                                                                                                       
  ├──────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────┤
  │ P(2nd red | 1st red) │ Just 3/9                          │ 33.3%              │                                                                                                       
  └──────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────┘  
  how is it different to say both balls are red vs 2nd red given 1st is also red. Both appears to mean we want 1st ans 2nd to be Red                                                                                     

● Great question — this is the core of conditional probability. Both situations do end up with two red balls, but the difference is what you already know when you're asking the question.

  ---
  P(both red) — asked before any draw

  You're standing in front of the bag. Nothing has happened yet. You're asking:

  ▎ "What are the chances I get lucky twice in a row?"

  Both draws are uncertain. You could draw blue first. You could draw blue second. You need to account for ALL of that uncertainty.

  P(2nd red | 1st red) — asked after the first draw

  The first ball has already been pulled out, and you're holding a red ball in your hand. That uncertainty is gone. Now you're asking:

  ▎ "The first one is done — it was red. Now what are my chances on this next draw?"

  ---
  The math shows the difference:

  P(both red)          = 4/10  ×  3/9  =  12/90  ≈ 13.3%
  P(2nd red | 1st red) =            3/9  =  30/90  ≈ 33.3%

  P(both red) is much smaller because it also bakes in the 6/10 chance the first ball could have been blue. The conditional strips that out — it says "forget the first draw, it's settled."

  ---
  Analogy: Imagine a friend bets you $10 that you'll flip heads twice in a row.

  - Before any flip: your chance is 1/4 (25%) — that's the joint probability
  - After you've already flipped heads once: your chance is now 1/2 (50%) — that's the conditional
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  Same two flips, same outcome (heads + heads), but completely different question depending on when you're asking.

  Q4 (harder). A factory has two machines:
  - Machine X produces 60% of items, with a 2% defect rate                                                                                                                                
  - Machine Y produces 40% of items, with a 5% defect rate
                                                                                                                                                                                          
  An item is picked at random and found to be defective. What is the probability it came from Machine Y?   

  P(X) = 0.6 P(D|X) = 0.02 
  P(Y) = 0.4 P(D|Y) = 0.05    

  P(Y|D) = ?    

  P(Y and  D)  = P(D)P(Y|D)   

  P(D) = P(D|X)*P(X) +   P(D|Y)*P(y)   = 0.02*.6 + 0.05* .4 = 0.032
  P(Y and D) = P(Y)  * P(D|Y) = P(D) * P(Y|D)      
             => 0.400 * 0.05 = 0.032  * P(Y|D)
             => P(Y|D) = (400*0.05)/32= 62.5  

  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  What you need is Bayes' Theorem:                                                                                                                                                                                       
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  P(Y|D) = P(Y and D) / P(D)
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  You have the numerator. The missing piece is P(D) — the total probability of picking a defective item from either machine.                                                                                             
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  Step 1 — P(D) using Total Probability:
  P(D) = P(D|X)×P(X)  +  P(D|Y)×P(Y)                                                                                                                                                                                     
       = 0.02 × 0.6   +  0.05 × 0.4 
       = 0.012         +  0.020                                                                                                                                                                                          
       = 0.032
  So 3.2% of all items are defective.                                                                                                                                                                                    
                                     
  Step 2 — Apply Bayes':                                                                                                                                                                                                 
  P(Y|D) = P(Y) × P(D|Y)  /  P(D)                                                                                                                                                                                        
          =    0.020        /  0.032
          =   62.5%                                                                                                                                                                                                      
                  
  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  Intuition check: Machine Y only makes 40% of items, but its defect rate (5%) is much higher than X's (2%). 
  So when you find a defective item, it's more likely to have come from Y than its 40% share suggests — 62.5%
  vs 40%. The high defect rate "pulls" the blame toward Y.                                                                                                                                                               
                  
  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  The pattern for all Bayes questions:                                                                                                                                                                                 
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  P(cause | effect) = P(cause) × P(effect | cause)
                      ─────────────────────────────                                                                                                                                                                      
                             P(effect)                                                                                                                                                                                 
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  Where P(effect) = sum over all causes of P(cause) × P(effect | cause).

  Q1. Medical Test (classic)
  A disease affects 1% of the population.
  A test for it is 95% accurate if you have the disease.
  The test also has a 5% false positive rate (flags healthy people as sick).
  You test positive. What is the probability you actually have the disease?

  P(Disease) = Prior = 0.01                                                                                                                                                                                              
  P(No Disease) = complement = 0.99                                                                                                                                                                                      
  P(Test Positive | Disease) = Likelihood = 0.95 (true positive)                                                                                                                                                         
  P(Test Positive | don't have disease) = 0.05 (false positive)                                                                                                                                                          
  P(Disease | Test positive) = Posterior = ?                                                                                                                                                                             
  =[ P(disease) *P(Test positive | have disease)] / P(Test Positive)]                                                                                                                                                    
  = (0.01*0.95)/(0.95*0.01 + 0.05*0.99)                                                                                                                                                                                  
  where, P(Test positive) = P(Test Positive | Disease) * P(Disease) + P(Test Positive | don't have disease) * P(Don't have disease)     = 16.1%

 Because even though the false positive rate is only 5%, there are so many healthy people that a small percentage of a large group overwhelms a large percentage of a tiny group.                                       
                                                            
  Think of it concretely with 10,000 people:                                                                                                                                                                             
   
  Sick people    (1%  of 10,000) =   100                                                                                                                                                                                 
  Healthy people (99% of 10,000) = 9,900                                                                                                                                                                                 
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  True positives:  100  × 0.95 =    95  (sick and test positive)                                                                                                                                                         
  False positives: 9900 × 0.05 =   495  (healthy but test positive)                                                                                                                                                      
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  Total positives = 95 + 495 = 590                                                                                                                                                                                       
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  P(actually sick | tested positive) = 95 / 590 = 16.1%                                                                                                                                                                  
● Because even though the false positive rate is only 5%, there are so many healthy people that a small percentage of a large group overwhelms a large percentage of a tiny group.

  Think of it concretely with 10,000 people:

  Sick people    (1%  of 10,000) =   100
  Healthy people (99% of 10,000) = 9,900

  True positives:  100  × 0.95 =    95  (sick and test positive)
  False positives: 9900 × 0.05 =   495  (healthy but test positive)

  Total positives = 95 + 495 = 590

  P(actually sick | tested positive) = 95 / 590 = 16.1%

  So when you test positive, you're in a pool of 590 people. Only 95 of them are actually sick. The other 495 are false alarms — healthy people caught by that 5% error rate.

  The key lesson — Base Rate Neglect:
  People hear "95% accurate" and assume a positive test means ~95% chance you're sick. But the prior (only 1% have the disease) dominates. 
  The rarity of the disease means false positives vastly outnumber true positives.

  This is why doctors order a second confirmatory test after a positive result — Bayes tells you to update again with the new evidence.

  ========================
  HOW BAYES DIFFERS FROM OTHER PROBABILITY
  ========================

  The four types — what question each answers:

    Marginal:     P(Train Late) = 75/500          "How often does X happen?"
    Joint:        P(Raining AND Late) = 45/500    "How often do both happen together?"
    Conditional:  P(Late | Raining) = 45/100      "Given I know something, how likely is X?"
    Bayes:        P(Raining | Late) = 45/75       "I see the effect — what caused it?"

  Marginal, joint, and conditional move FORWARD — you know the situation, ask about outcome.
  Bayes moves BACKWARD — you see the outcome, ask what caused it.

  ● In the weather example:

  Effect  =  Train is Late        ← this is what you observed                                                                                                                                                            
  Cause   =  Was it Raining?      ← this is what you're working backwards to find                                                                                                                                        
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  So P(Raining | Late) reads as:                                                                                                                                                                                         
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  ▎ "The train is already late. What is the probability that rain caused it?"                                                                                                                                            
                                                            
  You're not predicting whether the train will be late. It's already late. You're now investigating — looking back at what most likely produced that outcome.                                                            
                                                            
  Same pattern across all your exercises:                                                                                                                                                                                
                                                            
  Medical:  Effect = positive test result    Cause = do I actually have the disease?
  Factory:  Effect = defective item          Cause = which machine made it?                                                                                                                                              
  Spam:     Effect = email contains "FREE"   Cause = is it spam?

  ---
  The key difference — same exercises, opposite direction:

  ┌──────────────┬──────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Exercise     │ Forward question                 │ Bayes question                      │
  ├──────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Factory      │ Machine X has 2% defect rate     │ Item IS defective — which machine?  │
  │ Medical      │ Test is 95% accurate             │ Test IS positive — do I have it?    │
  │ Spam         │ 90% of spam has "FREE"           │ Email HAS "FREE" — is it spam?      │
  │ Cricket      │ Sachin's century rate            │ India DID win — did Sachin century? │
  │ Weather      │ Rain causes 45% late rate        │ Train IS late — was it raining?     │
  └──────────────┴──────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────┘

  ---
  Why Bayes needs the prior (and others don't):

  Conditional P(Late | Raining) only needs the rain rows — doesn't care how often it rains.
  Bayes P(Raining | Late) needs to know how often it rains — because you're looking at the
  late column and asking how much was caused by rain vs other reasons. Without the prior,
  you can't split the blame.

  This is why P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A):
    P(Late | Raining) = 45/100 = 45%   ← of rainy days, how often late
    P(Raining | Late) = 45/75  = 60%   ← of late days, how often rainy
  Same cell, completely different question.

  ---
  The formula is just this logic written down:

    P(Cause | Effect) =        what cause contributes to this effect
                        ────────────────────────────────────────────
                           total ways this effect could have happened

  The denominator (total probability) is what makes Bayes different — it forces you to
  account for ALL possible causes before assigning credit to one.

  ---
  One line summary:

  All other probability tells you how likely something is going forward.
  Bayes tells you — given something already happened — what most likely caused it.

  Everything you've learned maps directly into ML. Here's how:                                                                                                                                                           
                                                                                                                                                                                                                       
  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  1. Naive Bayes Classifier — your spam filter, scaled up

  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  2. Training a model = updating the prior with data        
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  Every ML model is doing a version of:
  Prior belief (before data)  →  see training data  →  Posterior belief (your model)                                                                                                                                     
  The model starts with some assumption, sees examples, and shifts its belief — exactly what Bayes describes.
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  3. Class imbalance = the medical test problem                                                                                                                                                                          
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  Your Q1 showed that 95% test accuracy means only 16% posterior when disease is rare. In ML, this is the class imbalance problem:
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  - Fraud detection: 99.9% transactions are legit                                                                                                                                                                        
  - Cancer screening: most patients are healthy                                                                                                                                                                          
                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
  A model that ignores the prior (base rate) will look accurate but fail completely on the rare class — same trap as ignoring the 1% disease prevalence.                                                                 
   
  ---                                                                                                                                                                                                                    
  4. What the model output actually means                   
                                         
  When a model says "80% probability this is a cat" — that IS a posterior:
  P(cat | pixel values) = P(cat) × P(pixels | cat) / P(pixels)                                                                                                                                                           
  The model has learned the likelihoods from training data and combines them with base rates to give you that number.
